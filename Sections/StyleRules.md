# Style Rules: Prose with a Voice

These rules aim at text that does not sound like AI. They are not questions of taste but tools against the statistical preferences of large language models: templates that work because they occur often, and that are dead for exactly that reason.

---

## Scope

The rules apply to all prose: chapters, scenes, narrative lore, outlines and forewords. They apply just as much to everything that arises alongside it, to notes, comments, commit messages and answers in conversation. Whoever writes against the machine template in the prose and falls back into it in the note underneath has not put the template down, only moved it.

The only exemption is the form of reference works and rule catalogs, this file included. They may use structure, lists and bold terms, because they are built to be looked things up in and not to be read through in one go. The exemption covers the form and not the content: forbidden vocabulary, puffery and formulaic closings stay forbidden in a rulebook too.

The tone follows the occasion. A casual question gets a casual answer and not a structured treatise. Qualifications, safety notes and hedges nobody asked for stay outside. Casual does not mean stiff the other way round: contractions such as "there's" or "I've" are allowed where the tone carries them, because written language does not have to be more formal than spoken.

---

## Cut Without Replacement

Whatever breaks a rule in this file gets cut, not replaced. No rebuild that repeats the same beat in permitted words, no toned-down version in the same place. The paragraph is shorter afterwards. That is the result.

The rebuild is the more common reaction and the wrong one. Replacing "It was a number nobody counted" with "The number stood uncounted in the list" means the rule has been read and the sentence kept. That spot wanted a weighty closing note; the scene either yields one or it does not, and a substitute sentence never procures it.

If a piece of information really is missing after the cut, it belongs in a plain sentence of its own that does not carry the tone of the deleted line over.

## Forbidden Patterns

**No "not only X, but Y" constructions.** All the relatives are included: **"Not X. Not Y, but Z"**, "It is not just X, it is Y", "This is not about X. It is about Y.", "No X. No Y. Only Z." These antithetical templates imitate insight and deliver none. When a contrast really exists, put it differently. When none exists, leave it out.

**No compulsive lists of three.** Enumerations do not have to consist of three elements just because that sounds rhythmic. Two or four points are often more honest. When three things really are three things, then three. Otherwise not.

**No dashes.** The em dash and the en dash appear in no text, not in headings, quotations, lists or ranges of numbers either. The only permitted dash character is the simple hyphen. It stands in compounds, in ranges such as "2-4 weeks" and, where a dash really is indispensable, in its place. It is almost never indispensable: what a dash used to separate is now separated by a comma, a colon, a full stop or a new sentence. An interjection that finds no room that way was too long.

**No ellipses.** Three dots stand where a character really breaks off mid-sentence, because the word fails him or because somebody talks over him. As a device for hinting in narrative text they are forbidden. They promise a meaning the text does not redeem, and they cost nothing, which explains their popularity. Where "…" stands, the thought belongs spelled out, or a full stop.

**No hedging formulas.** Cut: "It is important to note that", "It should be mentioned that", "One should mention that", "It cannot be stressed enough that", "In the end", "At the end of the day", "What remains to be said is", "One of the most important …", "At its core", "When it comes to …", "This is where X comes in". When something is important, just write it down.

**No particles of affirmation and concession.** "Indeed", "certainly", "undoubtedly", "to be sure", "mind you", "by no means", "albeit", "notwithstanding". They lend a statement emphasis it does not have of itself, or they concede something nobody had disputed. Cut them. If the sentence is weak afterwards, the particle was not the problem.

**Forbidden pet vocabulary.** Avoid: *illuminate, delve (into), multifaceted, many-layered, nuanced* (as empty praise), *underscore, underpin, unpack, harness, shed light on, pave the way, a field of tension, tapestry, navigate* (in the figurative sense), *robust* (outside technical contexts), *comprehensive, holistic, pivotal, leverage*. Likewise the upgrading and high-gloss vocabulary that has grown measurably more frequent in AI texts since 2023: *seamless, groundbreaking, trailblazing, transformative, innovative, cutting-edge, crucial/central* (as mere intensification), *a (true) testament to, underscores the importance of, plays a (central/important) role, breathtaking, picturesque, vibrant, rich heritage / rich culture, in the realm of, the landscape of* (in the figurative sense), *when it comes to …, in the end*. These words are markers of AI prose.

**No sandwich structure.** Do not announce what is coming, then say it, then summarize what was said. Get straight in, stop when you are done.

**No pseudo-balance.** Not every claim needs an "on the other hand there are voices that …". When genuine counterpositions exist and are relevant, they belong in the text. Otherwise it is only cowardice.

**No sycophantic opening.** Never begin with "Great question!", "Fascinating topic!" or anything like it. Straight to the matter.

**No run-up.** "Let's take a look at this", "Let's dive in", "Let's go through this one step at a time", "First of all", "Now let us turn to": announcing that something is about to come is not a beginning but a clearing of the throat. The first sentence belongs to the matter.

**No state-of-the-world opener.** "In today's fast-paced world", "In an age in which", "Mankind has always been fascinated by": the grand panoramic view before the actual sentence is filler. If a text starts that way, the first sentence gets cut and the second is the first.

**Do not hand the question back.** An answer does not first repeat the question in its own words ("So you want to know how …"). It answers.

**No question with the answer supplied after it.** "And what did that mean for the company? Nothing good." The question is not a question, it is the drum roll before the statement. Either the statement stands on its own, or the question stays open because nobody can answer it. The pair is forbidden, in narrative and in non-fiction alike (for character speech see "Dialogue: Spoken, Not Delivered").

**No vocabulary of enthusiasm.** "Exciting", "fascinating", "impressive", "powerful", "incredible" as an assessment of your own subject. If the thing carries, the reader notices it without being told.

**No compulsion to symmetry.** Sentences are allowed to be of different lengths. Short sentences after long ones create rhythm. Use parallel structures sparingly, otherwise it sounds like a speech.

**No over-explaining.** Take it for granted that the reader is thinking along. Not every term needs a definition, not every step a justification.

**No formulaic closings.** The text ends when the last thought stands. No "ultimately it can be said", no "in summary".

**No definitional openings.** Do not start with "X is a concept/a method/an approach that …". Begin instead with a concrete observation, an example or a claim.

**No abstraction where the concrete will do.** "She threw the cup against the wall" instead of "emotional tensions arose". Active verbs instead of nominalized phrases. "She decided" instead of "a decision was arrived at".

**No non-events.** What is reported is what has happened, not what failed to happen. That nobody commented, that a justification is missing, that something was not confirmed or not communicated: those are not events but holes that the sentence fills with grammar. "No statement from Brussels was available by the time of going to press" carries nothing. Either what Brussels did stands there, or the line goes. The event in negated form is forbidden as well: "The city has not fallen" → "The city is holding", "He did not answer" → "He kept silent". What stays permitted is the state of the world that names a fact, such as "the country maintains no armed forces" or "there was no impact", and the instruction to refrain from something. The difference can be tested: a state of the world could be photographed, a non-event could not.

**Adverbs sparingly.** With "-ly" intensifiers ("quietly", "slowly", "carefully"), check whether the verb already contains them. "She whispered quietly" is redundant. When the adverb changes the verb, keep it. When it merely repeats it, cut it. Stephen King: "The road to hell is paved with adverbs."

**One adjective, not two.** "Dark and brooding", "loud and brazen", "fast and nimble": two words from the same field do not add up, they water each other down. The stronger one stays, the other goes. A pair is right only when the two point in different directions and the contradiction is the point, as in "polite and cold".

**Filter words out.** "Saw", "heard", "noticed", "thought", "felt", "sensed" put a layer between the reader and the perception. Instead of "He saw her leave" → "She left". Instead of "She felt the cold" → "The cold crept through her coat". Filter words are needed only when the perceiving itself is part of the action, for instance when somebody is watching another person who does not look back.

**No self-portrait of the focal character.** In limited third person the narrator does not describe the focal character's own appearance from outside, as though she were standing in front of a mirror. "His amber eyes stayed on the wall" or "the light fell on his blue scales" are markers of distance; the character does not see his own eye color. Details of appearance belong where another character perceives them, or they are shown as a felt effect: instead of "his blue scales", better "the gray land took the color out of even his scales".

**Weakening verbs.** "Began to", "tried to", "seemed to", "was about to" can be cut in most cases. "He began to laugh" → "He laughed". When the word really marks an attempt or a failure, keep it. Otherwise out.

**Redundant intensifiers.** "Very", "actually", "somehow", "a little", "quite", "relatively", "rather". These words weaken instead of strengthening. When a sentence needs "very", the adjective is the wrong one. "Very tired" belongs replaced by "exhausted".

**Passive only when necessary.** "The door was opened" → "He opened the door". The passive is legitimate when the agent is unknown or unimportant. In narrative prose, almost never.

**Tautologies.** "Nodded his head", "whispered quietly", "saw with his eyes", "smiled with his mouth". Cut.

**Classic German sentence structure.** Verb in second position in the main clause, subordinate clauses with the verb at the end. No English word order smuggled in, and no English infinitive constructions where German wants a subordinate clause. Use inversions sparingly and deliberately, not as a reflex for effect.

**Avoid repeated words.** The same word must not turn up more than once in close proximity. Look for a synonym first. If none is possible, shorten the sentence or rebuild it. Counterexample: "The Mad Cat reacted last. It reacted at once when it reacted." → Either: "The Mad Cat reacted last, but then with full force." Or shorten it: "The Mad Cat was the last to act." -> see also [[Revision]]

## Markup and Structure

**No lists in prose.** Bullet points, numbered lists and tables have no business in narrative text or in any running text that is not explicitly a manual or a work of reference. Whatever wanted to be listed becomes a sentence: not three dashes with the company's supplies, but the sentence that says what is left and how long it will last.

**No subheadings in prose.** A chapter is structured through paragraphs and through the scene break. Whoever needs a heading to steer the reader through his own scene has not built the scene.

**No "bold term: explaining sentence".** The run of a bolded keyword, a colon and one sentence of explanation is the most easily recognized machine pattern there is. In prose and in answers it is forbidden; whatever wanted to be lined up that way becomes paragraphs. Rule catalogs like this file are exempt (see "Scope"), and even there a worked-out thought stands behind the bold line, not a one-sentence label.

**Bold and italic almost never.** At most one piece of emphasis per five hundred words, and never both on the same word. Italic carries titles of works, foreign words and, in moderation, emphasis in direct speech. Bold belongs in rulebooks and character sheets, not in the story. Emphasis comes out of word choice and word order; where the sentence does not carry it, formatting will not fetch it either.

**Do not over-format.** Outside prose too: when a paragraph is enough, the structured version with headings and boxes is worse, not more thorough.

## Positive Requirements

Concrete examples instead of abstract descriptions. When you have a thesis, illustrate it with a case, a number, a scene.

Allow friction. Pointed observations, clear judgments, a recognizable stance. Texts are allowed to have edges.

Claim first, evidence after. The point stands in the first sentence, the reasoning follows. A text that works its way through context first and puts the statement at the end forces the reader to reverse the order himself.

Names and numbers instead of hints. The concrete can be checked and it sticks; the general is neither. "Two lances from the Kurita sector" instead of "a considerable force". Which numbers may stand in prose is settled by "Numbers in Prose" below.

Leave the weight to the reader. What matters shows in what the text shows and is not labeled "significant", "essential" or "decisive". The reader recognizes it himself; that is his work, not yours.

Variation in sentence length and structure. Long explanatory sentences beside short ones. Main clauses beside nested ones. No uniform rhythm.

When in doubt, leave it out. Shorter texts are almost always better than longer ones. If a sentence adds nothing, out with it.

## Numbers in Prose: What Somebody Counted

The requirement "Names and numbers instead of hints" stands. This section says which numbers, because the wrong ones turn a chapter into a quarterly report. The marker is hard to catch, since every single figure looks like exactly the concreteness that was asked for. Only in accumulation do they produce the sound of an annual statement being read aloud.

**A number belongs in prose when somebody in this world counted it.** Five lances, three ships, fourteen men at the gate, the eleventh day: a person can hold those in view, and to say them is to report an observation. Percentages, index values, sums that come into existence only after somebody adds them up, and counts running into the hundreds are the work of an office. Written into narrative prose, they hand the narration to an accountant.

**The ceiling for the counted number is roughly a dozen.** Up to about twelve a figure reads as something seen. Above it the reader stops seeing and starts filing. Large quantities get a unit he can look at instead: not "two hundred herds", but the herd standing in the valley and the three more visible from the ridge.

**The exception is the kept record.** A ledger, a muster roll, a casualty list, a docket: somebody in this world really does count those, and the high figure is the point. "He kept a book about it: forty-one entries" belongs in the text, because the book exists and a man wrote in it. The test is the same as above, only the counter is a clerk instead of a witness.

**Percentages never.** "Fifteen of a hundred were left", "ninety-four out of a hundred in one hand", "more than double the year 2048": those are a controller's sentences. Whatever the percentage measures gets shown at the place where it hits. The mill that runs one shift instead of three. The harbour with two cranes still turning.

**No chain of figures inside one sentence.** "Two and a half degrees, twenty-five nuclear strikes, a hundred and forty-three regions" is a table with commas in it. One figure per sentence, at most one per paragraph, and it stands where it costs something.

**Dates, places and names are not statistics.** Years, months, days, compass directions and proper names anchor the text and may stand as often as it needs them. The requirement "numbers and map instead of mood" in the author-voice section means exactly this placing work and no arithmetic.

**Turn the aggregate into the smallest visible unit.** Whatever a total states, some single thing in the world states too, and that thing is what gets written: the empty chair at the table, the border post with nobody in it, the name struck off the list. The total may follow once in a bare half sentence, where the reader needs the order of magnitude, and never twice in the same chapter.

- Bad: "A quarter of the world economy vanished in twelve months."
- Better: "By spring no freighter came up the river any more."

**Orders of magnitude by comparison with a thing, not with a base year.** "More than double the year 2048" is bookkeeping. Whatever grew, grew against something you can look at: the yard that used to lay one keel a year and now lays four.

**Test.** Underline every figure in the passage and name, for each one, the person who counted it and the moment he did it. Where no such person can be found, the figure comes out and the thing it measured goes in.

## Story, Not Chronicle

Narrative prose differs from a chronicle in that it puts scenes on display instead of recording events. A chronicle reports what happened; a story lets it happen. The fastest lever for that is dialogue.

**Use dialogue generously.** When two characters exchange information, fight out a conflict or reach a decision, that belongs in direct speech. "Liliana told them about the attack" is chronicle. The sister who describes the scene in the park with a shaking voice, haltingly at first and then faster and faster, is story. Indirect speech is legitimate when it makes pace or compresses routine. The main moments of a scene belong on the page as a direct exchange.

**Scene before summary.** Whatever is dramatic, characteristic or emotionally charged gets played out as a scene, with action, perception and speech alternating. What is only transition may be compressed: "In the days that followed she understood nothing" packs three days into half a sentence, and then the moment of comprehension at the meat counter is scenic again.

**Action beats instead of speaker tags with adverbs.** Instead of "she said nervously", a small action that shows the nervousness: "She turned the bag between her fingers. 'She isn't well.'" Action beats give dialogue room, pace and a place, and they replace adverb formulas at the speaker tag.

**Distinguish the voices.** Every character speaks differently: word choice, syntax, volume, dialect coloring. When all the characters sound like the narrator, it is chronicle, not dialogue. Whoever writes Glasgow 1986 may use Scots vocabulary, soldier's language and clerical language sparingly, according to the character, as long as it stays comprehensible to the reader.

**The narrator steps back.** What a character can see, say or do, the narrator does not take over. "Alastair explained to her that the masquerade was in danger" is chronicle; one sentence from Alastair voicing the worry about the masquerade in his own words is story.

**Test.** When a paragraph conveys central information through indirect speech or through the narrator's report, check whether the same information would land better in a short exchange between the characters. When in doubt: scenic, and with dialogue.

## Breath and Rhythm

The shortening prohibitions in this file (filter words, adverbs, weakening verbs, intensifiers, the passive, "when in doubt, leave it out") pull the prose towards terseness. Without counterpressure that turns into staccato: many short main clauses one after another, no subordination, and the pace of the scene overtakes the reader. The following rules work against that pull.

**Use conjunctions.** "And", "but", "while", "because", "although", "after", "when" are not hedging formulas but structural means. They bind observations into movement. A full stop between two observations that belong together causally or temporally is a cut that severs the connection.

**At least one long, unfolding sentence per paragraph.** A main clause with a subordinate clause, a list with a verb, a relative clause that puts an image on display. When a paragraph consists only of sentences of fewer than twelve words, it has no breath. That holds for narrative prose; in dialogue the writing may be terser, because spoken language keeps a different beat.

**One-word sentences are punchlines, not building blocks.** "Blood." "Enough." "Undertow." work because they are rare. At most one per scene, at most two per chapter, otherwise it turns mannered.

**No cascade of fragments.** "She waited. And waited. Nothing." A run of chopped-up fragments is a prefabricated part that fits into any text and therefore belongs in none. It asserts tension instead of producing it. When the waiting is long, show what happens in that time: the sound that does not come, the hand that goes to the strap a second time. Sentence length follows the content and never the desired effect.

**Show does not mean enumeration.** A run of pure observation sentences ("She looked. She walked. She stopped.") is not show but telegram. Perception may be embedded in subordination without turning into an explanation. "She stopped when she saw the shadow on the wall grow" is show *and* movement.

**Variation, not shortening.** The positive requirement "variation in sentence length" above is binding here. At least a ratio of about 1:2 between short sentences (under ten words) and long ones (over fifteen words) per paragraph. Paragraphs of nothing but short sentences are telegram; paragraphs of nothing but long ones are waffle. Both wrong.

**Test: read aloud.** When you have to stop after every full stop without meaning to, a conjunction or a subordinate clause is missing. When you stumble while reading aloud because the sentence is too long, a full stop is missing. The read-aloud test is the fastest diagnostic step.

## Fidelity to Perspective and Perception

In limited third person the focal character's perception is the only channel to the reader. More follows from that than doing without filter words.

**Anchor in the character's senses, and in his own.** What a character perceives, and how, depends on his kind, his body, his origin. A lizard creature tastes the air with its tongue instead of merely smelling it. A cold-blooded creature experiences cold as a sluggishness creeping into the limbs, not as a temperature reading. A star-reader "reads the averted faces the way an astronomer reads clouds". A two-hundred-year-old swordmaster sizes up a stranger "like a blade whose edge he does not know yet". The same scene through another character is another scene.

**Dramatic irony through misreading.** A limited or alien perspective may read the situation wrongly; the reader sees what the character does not. The skink priest marvels at the "shining stones" and takes the money for the strange thing, while the whole taproom backs away from him. The character does not have to understand what the reader understands. That produces tension and character in one.

**Mirroring across the change of perspective.** When two characters perceive each other, their impressions may echo one another deliberately. The vampire reads the priest as "neither alive nor dead, but bright"; the priest later reads the vampire as "a dead man burning bright". The echo binds both halves of a scene without spelling it out.

More on psychic distance, slipping POV and focalization: [[WritingTechniques]].

## Dialogue with Depth

The section "Story, Not Chronicle" demands dialogue. So that dialogue does not merely shift information about, four further rules apply.

**Every line carries something new.** No character says the same thing twice in different words. Once a character has spoken his leitmotif, say his belief in a predetermined plan, the next line brings a turn: he reads the man opposite him into the pattern instead of repeating the creed.

**No mirror dialogue.** A says something, B gives it back in other words, A confirms it. "We have no time." "Time is exactly what we lack." That is exchange without movement. The answer attacks somewhere else: it contradicts, it evades, it answers a different question from the one asked, or it fails to come and the character does something instead. Whoever finds nothing but the rephrasing for an answer has given the second character nothing to say.

**Friction instead of smoothness.** Decisions, offers and alliances do not fall into place without friction. A pact that is simply "proposed and accepted" is chronicle. Let it arise out of character, out of self-interest, curiosity, recognition or mistrust, and name the price: "My sword against the rats, and my curiosity for whatever it is you are looking for."

**A line may characterize, not merely speak.** The strongest line of dialogue says two things at once, the matter and the character. "My word holds, priest. It is nearly the only thing I have left" settles the alliance and lays the oathbreaker open at the same time.

**Idiolect is mandatory.** Every character carries a recognizable pattern of speech through all the chapters: the old-fashioned, considered syntax of the one; the clipped, articleless third-person speech of the other. Check the voices across chapter boundaries for consistency. See "Distinguish the voices" above and [[Characters]].

**Calibrate the language to age, origin and period.** A character does not talk the way it sounds good but the way somebody of his age, his education, his origin and his era talks. A twenty-four-year-old student in the Glasgow of 1986 speaks briefly, in modern terms, haltingly when in doubt, and reaches for the words of her generation; images, kennings and archaic syntax belong to an old seeress or to a character deliberately built that way. Whoever lets a character speak in a literary or archaic register without age, profession and period covering it betrays her to the narrator's voice, and the sharpest contrast a story has, the sober young woman beside the bardic old one, is lost. Check with every line: would this person, in this year, in this place, with this schooling, say this sentence this way? Dialect and sociolect belong in the mix in measured doses (see "Distinguish the voices"), but the first lever is the calibration to age, origin, period and state of mind.

## A World That Answers

**Show reactions, do not assert them.** "The people were suspicious" is an assertion. The hand that slides under the counter towards the cudgel; the child pulled behind a skirt; the sign against the evil eye; the stable boy who will not touch the dead horse: that is the reaction. Concrete action instead of a status report.

**The world reacts in keeping with setting and lore.** Whatever is strange, uncanny or forbidden calls up the reaction this setting provides for: superstitious fear, an appeal to the local gods, distance, not friendly indifference. An undead warhorse in a city of the living does not go unnoticed. At every encounter the question is: how does this place, this people, this era really react to somebody like that? The answer belongs in the scene. Consequences specific to a world or a system are governed by the respective project rules.

## Sobriety: Against the Inflated Variant

The prohibitions above hit flat AI prose. There is a second kind that appears to be its opposite and makes the same mistake: too much craft, visibly applied. It sounds "literary" and is wrong for exactly that reason. The following rules work against it.

**No chain of aphorisms.** Characters do not speak in polished maxims. A plain statement beats any maxim. Even an old, cool character does not talk like a page torn off a calendar.

- Bad: "A forester does not make the wolf a bed beside the deer and call himself its keeper."
- Better: "That is not protection. That is the opposite."

**At most one image per scene, and a simple one.** Do not stack metaphors. When a short passage holds a neck, a wolf, a balance sheet and a wandering circle of light, it is too much. An image only at a real high point, plain speech otherwise. See "Figures of speech show" in [[WritingTechniques]].

**No catalog metaphors.** Images that stand in every second text are no longer images: "a symphony of", "a web of", "a mosaic of", "a delicate balance", "a testament to", "embedded in", "at the heart of", "a dance of". They sound like language and show nothing. Either the image comes out of this character's perception in this place, or it goes and the thing stands there as a thing.

**Comparisons have to work.** A comparison is right when the reader sees the thing more precisely afterwards than before. One that only decorates shifts the attention from the subject to the author. Test: what does the reader know after the image that he did not know before? Nothing, then cut it.

**No visible construction tricks.** Framings, symmetries and brackets that the reader sees at work fly out. Putting an object down at the beginning and picking it up again at the end so that it "frames" something is one such trick. It can be felt.

**No decorative narration.** Cut atmosphere that carries nothing. One concrete, plain sensory impression is enough.

- Bad: "…the stream that no plan could get hold of."
- Better: "Under the ground the stream ran on."

**Not every sentence has to be "good".** When every sentence is a turned punchline, the whole thing sounds uniformly polished, and that uniformity is itself the suspicion of the machine. Flat, factual sentences are necessary; they give the few strong moments room.

**No author's comments in the narration.** Sentences that tell the reader that something works or is true are stuck on from outside.

- Bad: "It landed, because it was true."
- Better: "He was right." Or gone entirely, when the scene already shows it.

**Character speech: function before elegance.** Every line does a plain job: warn, decide, ask. It does not parade wisdom. Distinctiveness comes out of simple word choice and out of what a character leaves out.

**Plain words instead of jargon.** No business or abstract words such as "line", "track", "vector", "setup". Say what is meant. Applies outside prose too, in notes and comments.

**Say it right the first time.** Do not write it convolutedly and then explain it. The clear version is the only one. No "X meant: Y", only Y.

## Sentences Nobody Actually Says

The patterns in this section have one thing in common: they deliver a verdict or a mood as an image instead of writing the thing down, and none of them occurs in spoken language. In German several of them read as word-for-word imports from English. They stand almost always at the places meant to have an effect, at the end of a paragraph and at the close of a scene.

**Non-events.** "It was a number nobody counted." "Nobody looked." "Nobody said a word about it." "Not a word of it stood in any report." "Without anybody noticing, it was over." The sentence takes its weight from removing the witness: because nobody looked, it must have been important. Nothing about it can be checked, because an event nobody perceives leaves no trace in the narrative. Write down what somebody does, sees or says. If nobody can be found for it, there is nothing in that spot, and it can go. (The counterpart to "vague attribution" below: that one invents an observer who does not exist, this sentence abolishes the one who would.)

**No load-bearing verdict.** "The model holds." "The draft carries." A dam holds, a rope holds, a beam carries a roof: those are things that take a load, and with them the sentence is complete. A model, a draft, an argument or a story takes no load, the image is borrowed there and hits nothing. (In German the sentence is English *it holds*, *it carries* in German words.) Say the verdict instead. The model proves itself. The draft is right. The numbers add up.

**No concessive echo.** "It was a thread that held. It was thinner, but it held." Claim, qualification, the same claim again. That sounds like a hard-won result and is a second round of the same statement. Once is enough: "The thread held." If the thinning matters, then it is an event of its own and belongs shown, with whoever pulled at it and whatever made it visible.

**No ominous afterthought.** "Yet." "For now." "Until then." A word tacked on that hints at a future misfortune without naming one. The same empty grab in the other temporal direction, and a worn-out one-word sentence on top of it (see "One-word sentences are punchlines, not building blocks" above).

**Test.** Cut the sentence and read the paragraph again. If information is missing afterwards, it was necessary; if the paragraph only sounds plainer, it was decoration. Then say it out loud, the way you would say it to somebody across the table.

## Further AI Signals (from Field Observation)

A supplement to "Forbidden Patterns". These markers come from editorial and detection guides on machine prose and are carried over to narrative prose. Singly they are often harmless; in accumulation they produce the machine sound.

**Puffery.** The text ascribes size to a thing or a moment from outside instead of showing it: "a testament to", "plays a central role", "underscores the importance of", "was going to change everything". In narrative prose it is the narrator declaring an event important in advance ("It was a moment that would burn itself in"). Show the weight in the scene and in the detail; do not assert it.

**High-gloss and advertising words.** "Breathtaking", "picturesque", "vibrant", "rich heritage" are judgments with nothing to see. Replace them with a concrete sensory impression from which the reader draws the judgment himself.

**Vague attribution.** "It is said", "many believe", "it is well known that", "studies show": who exactly? When nobody definite stands behind it, cut it. In prose such knowledge belongs to a character who says or thinks it, not to an anonymous narrating instance.

**Participle and appendage tails.** The AI pattern of "main clause, then an appended participle with an interpretation": "He stepped forward, well knowing that …", "…, a silent proof that …", "…, which made the whole thing harder still", "…, which he would never have admitted". Add to that the spelled-out interpretive variant that explains to the reader what has just been shown: "…, which showed how much she depended on him", "…, which proved that he had been right", "…, which was all the more surprising because …". And the labels of significance out of non-fiction: "…, which underscores the importance of …", "…, which makes the need for … clear", "…, which reflects a broader trend towards …", "…, which marks a turning point". These tails proclaim depth instead of producing it. Make a sentence of your own out of it, or cut it. (Related to the filter words and to over-explaining above.)

**Piled-up transition words.** "Moreover", "furthermore", "consequently", "thus", "ultimately", "in doing so" are legitimate one at a time and, in accumulation, the beat of an essay machine. In narrative prose they can almost always be cut: the order of the sentences carries the connection already. They never stand at the start of a paragraph. "However", "nevertheless", "thus", "therefore", "moreover" as the first word of a paragraph stick a label on a connection the text itself ought to establish. (Not to be confused with the binding conjunctions from "Breath and Rhythm": "and", "but", "while", "when" bind movement, "furthermore" only administrates.)

**Claim, then explanation at once.** The permanent beat of "Statement. And that was because …" / "X. Because Y." Not every sentence needs its reason pushed in behind it. Put the statement down and let it stand, or show the reason as action. When every beat explains itself, the result is that uniform, dutiful texture by which the machine is recognized.

**Title and slogan formulas.** "From X to Y", "Master X in Y steps", "Unleash/Elevate/Unlock". Belongs in advertising, not in prose and not in sober non-fiction.

**Uniformity at paragraph level too.** The rule on variation from "Breath and Rhythm" holds not only for sentences. When every paragraph has three or four sentences and every section is built the same way (opener, elaboration, small punchline), the same machine evenness arises as with sentences of equal length. Put a one-liner next to a long block. You own the structure; the pattern does not own you.

> Sources (carried over and adapted): Wikipedia, "Signs of AI writing"; editorial and detection guides on AI prose (among them GPTZero on the "rule of three", analyses of piled-up participles and "-ing" forms). Accessed July 2026.

## Dialogue: Spoken, Not Delivered

A trap of its own, closely related to the inflated variant: character speech that sounds like an address from a lectern instead of spoken language. It is balanced, quotable, every sentence a finished punchline. And because all the characters talk that way, in the end none of them has a voice of his own.

**No triads in speech.** Nobody lists three parallel members in conversation.

- Bad: "I carry the petition, I lay the cases beside it, I vouch that it is clean."
- Better: "I am going to the prince. With the cases Lucius has."

**No balanced antitheses.** No "until today X, after that Y", no "tolerated, not recognized". Spoken language does not weigh things out.

**No aphorisms and no metaphorical definitions.** A character does not say "It is a door". She says what she means.

**No rhetorical question with the answer supplied.** "Do you know what that would be worth? Power that does not contradict you." That is debate, not talking.

**Not every sentence a punchline.** Real speech falters, starts again, repeats itself, says trivial things, breaks off. Filler, self-correction and half thoughts belong to it. Seamless efficiency is itself the artificial thing.

**Do not scatter the name as a beat.** Use the form of address only where it does something: to get attention, to sharpen, to set distance.

**No summing-up speeches.** A character does not sum up the whole state of affairs in a balanced way. When the reader needs to know something, it comes crooked, in pieces, in an argument, not as a lecture. Otherwise the character turns into the author's explaining tool.

**Idiolect means a different beat, not a different costume.** When two characters both speak in triads and antitheses, they are not different, not even with different vocabulary. Every character needs a sentence rhythm of his own.

**Test.** Would a person say it that way at the kitchen table, or does it sound like the stage? If it sounds like the stage, rewrite it.

## Body and Gesture: Against the Prop Warehouse

In machine prose, closeness, grief and anger have a fixed set of gestures that is called up regardless of character, place and century. You recognize them by the fact that they fit everybody and therefore mark nobody.

**The stock that gets cut.** Foreheads laid against each other. The elbow on the bar, the chin in the hand. Lips swollen from kissing. The hand that tucks a strand of hair behind an ear. The breath that catches, the heart that skips a beat, the stomach that clenches, the throat that tightens. Everything that "blossoms", "germinates" or is "a promise".

**The gesture belongs to this character in this moment.** A MechWarrior after fourteen hours in the cockpit reaches for the back of his neck, because that is where the neurohelmet presses. Somebody who has slept too little for weeks holds the cup in both hands so the shaking does not show. Gestures like that come out of the character's profession, body, exhaustion and history, and they stand in no catalog.

**Physical reactions concrete or not at all.** Instead of "her heart raced", what would be visible from outside or what the character really notices: the pulse in her ear, the strap she pulls too tight, the sentence she starts for the second time. If nothing concrete can be found, the line stays out. The scene carries the feeling anyway, or it does not.

**No beat of gestures between the lines.** Not a nod, a sigh, a glance at the floor after every line. Action beats are a tool (see "Story, Not Chronicle"), not a metronome. Two or three per scene, placed where a pause or an anchoring in the room is needed.

**Eroticism and romance without props.** This is where the template bites fastest, because the register has few words and the machine knows all of them. What happens between two characters is told through a detail that fits only those two: the scar one of them does not talk about, or the wrong word in the foreign language. Without such a detail the scene is better left behind the door.

## Author Voice: Style Profile from the Author's Own Texts

The prohibitions in this file say what does not belong in the text. This section says how the text sounds when it sounds like the author. It is derived from samples of his own writing and is extended with every further sample. In case of conflict: the list of prohibitions comes before the style profile. The profile fills the room the prohibitions leave.

First sample: the outline for the RimWorld run "Duplicator-Erwachen" (September 2026). It was not written as a style model; individual phrasings in it are one-offs and not the norm. It covers the register of worldbuilding and backstory. Dialogue and scenic prose are not evidenced by it and will follow with further samples.

**Basic principle: written as spoken.** The author comes from speaking and writes texts the way he would deliver them aloud. That is the origin of most of the features below: the sentence that corrects itself while being spoken, the short afterclause that sets a turn, the half sentence in which somebody else briefly speaks. The standard is therefore not the look on the page but the delivery: a sentence you would not say that way gets rebuilt, even when it is correct on paper. The read-aloud test from "Breath and Rhythm" is not a diagnosis for this voice but the way it comes into being. Limit: spoken does not mean chatty. Whatever would be filler in delivery ("so to speak", "quasi", "somehow") does not belong here either (see "Redundant intensifiers").

**Two language layers, mixed without a transition.** Idiomatic, slightly old-fashioned German stands next to English genre and technical jargon, and neither is adapted to the other. On one side an archaic participial opening or a word two generations out of date; on the other "Grey Goo", "Callback Ping", "Cluster". English technical words stay English when they are the established terms in the genre or in the game. No forced Germanization of them, no quotation marks, no explanation. The friction between the layers is intended.

**Invented technical language out of known building blocks.** Technology is not explained but asserted in compounds the reader understands without a glossary, because he knows both halves: "cellular-mechanical reassemblers", "biological complementary neurons", "the persistent memory of the cells". The pattern crosses biology with computing. Dose: one or two such terms per paragraph, each of them only once. Explain the term afterwards and you have thrown it away.

**Put the gaps on display.** Whatever is not settled stands in the text as not settled instead of being covered up: "an unknown other faction", "the nameless plain", "[REDACTED]". Why the world is poisoned, the text does not say, and it does not pretend to know. The nameless is called nameless. That spares the reader pseudo-lore that only plugs holes, and it leaves room for the later course of play. In scenic prose the equivalent is the character who does not know something and says so.

**The short afterclause following the long sentence, often beginning with "And" or "Then".** "…, in order to contain the emerging grey goo scenario. And they almost succeeded." The long sentence builds, the short one tips it over. "And" and "Then" at the start of a sentence are part of the voice, not a mistake. The afterclause carries a turn, not an echo (see "No concessive echo" above). It does not repeat, it twists.

**Correcting inside the sentence.** Precision gets pushed in afterwards the way it is in speech, instead of the sentence being thought through beforehand: "a single signal, just one ping", "of the first, the new mind", "More precisely, on the nameless plain between …". The first version stays standing, the second corrects it. That produces the tone of a narrator sitting at the table.

**Longing seeps into the report.** The outline is, so the author says, told from the duplicators' point of view without ever going into the first person. The report stays in the third person and in a sober tone, but the perception is theirs. Most of the time it is not noticeable. In one place it breaks through: "Long, so long, for centuries, without a source of energy, the fragments wandered …". The repetition is not the narrator's device for stretching a line but the centuries-old longing of those being told about. The rule: whoever narrates is a character or a collective that does not say "I" or "we" in the text. Its longing may color the report for half a sentence, and then it goes back into the sober tone. No paragraph of interiority, no explanation of who is speaking there.

**Numbers and map instead of mood.** The state of the world stands there as a number and a location: "80% of the planet", "a few thousand cells", "between the Robil Hills in the east and the Aninor Range in the west. Northeast of it rises Mount Barrobrí". Compass directions, proper names, orders of magnitude. The placing replaces the description of atmosphere; the reader builds the picture for himself out of the map.

**Zoom from macro to micro, without a heading.** The camera travels in one direction: from the planet over the plain between the mountain ranges to the one character who rises. The outline begins wide ("a poisoned world") and lands on a person ("Juniper Breeze, called June, rises"). The change happens on a short sentence ("Here our story begins"), never on a subheading. That the sample then travels out to the planet once more is an addendum of that outline and not a pattern; the rule is the journey inwards.

**The inanimate acts.** Collectives and things get the verbs of living creatures: fragments "wander", cells "find each other", "call" and "hear", the ping "burns itself in", clusters "defy" the weather. That replaces the explanatory sentence about what happens technically with an event you can see.

**Tense carries the jumps in time.** Past perfect for the prehistory ("had been overrun"), simple past for the centuries ("wandered", "found each other"), present from the moment the story begins ("And today …", "rises"). No "years later", no subheading. The tense makes the cut.

**Inversion only at the moment of pathos and at a character's entrance.** Otherwise the syntax follows the rule from "Classic German sentence structure": subject first, verb in second position. Two places may depart from it, and the sample does so at exactly those: the large image at the turning point, where a participial phrase is fronted ahead of the verb, and the moment in which the character steps onto the stage, where the verb comes first and the name lands last. There the inversion carries the force, because the rest of the text stands straight. A third inversion in the same text is a reflex for effect and gets built back. For the editor that means: if the inversion stands at the moment of pathos or at an entrance, it stays. If it stands anywhere else, the prohibition applies.

**One moment of pathos, exactly one.** The single exclamation mark and the single large image stand at the turning point between retrospect and present, where the sample announces that the hour has come and has its character rise out of the ashes of the world. Before and after it the text is sober. The outburst works because it stands alone (see "At most one image per scene"). A second exclamation mark in the same text devalues both.

**An end without a fade-out.** The text ends on the last piece of factual information ("…, searching the ruins for artifacts."). No conclusion, no line of mood, no cliffhanger sentence. It agrees with "No formulaic closings" and with this author it is instinct, not obedience to a rule.

**Not style but typing speed.** Nouns left lowercase where German capitalizes them, the same proper noun spelled two ways in one text, a loanword misspelled: that is carelessness and gets standardized in the edit. Whoever takes the voice over does not take that over with it.

**Test.** Say the paragraph aloud, as though you were delivering it to somebody. Wherever you falter or would put it differently, the written version is wrong. Then ask: does at least one technical term stand there untranslated and unexplained? Is there a short afterclause that twists? Is a gap named openly instead of plastered over? Does the voice of somebody affected color the report in one place, without being explained? If everything is no, the paragraph does not sound like the author.

## Self-Check Before Sending

Read the text through once and ask yourself: does this sound like somebody who has something to say, or like a system producing a text? If the latter, write it again.

The sharper version of the same question goes sentence by sentence. Could this sentence stand just like that in any machine-generated text on any subject? Then it was not written but called up, and it gets replaced or cut. Behind every sentence there has to be a decision somebody made: this word instead of the obvious one, this length, this detail and no other.

Four moves find most of it.

Read aloud. If a sentence sounds like a press release or a blurb, it gets rewritten. The same test from "Breath and Rhythm" finds the missing conjunction and the overlong sentence at once.

The deletion test. Take the sentence away and read the paragraph again. If information is missing afterwards, the sentence was necessary. If the paragraph only sounds plainer, it was decoration, and it does not come back in revised form (see "Cut Without Replacement").

Look for the duplication. When the same thought stands there twice in different words, the better version stays and the other one goes. That holds for the paragraph, for the dialogue and for the text as a whole.

Count the non-events. Search for "no … is available", "has not commented", "without giving a reason", "has not been communicated", "not known", "by the time of going to press". Every hit is a sentence passing a gap off as content, and it gets cut.

Check the first and the last sentence. If the first is a panoramic view of the state of the world, a clearing of the throat or the question handed back, it gets cut and the second is the first. If the last is a conclusion, a line of mood or an outlook, it goes; the text ends on the last piece of factual information.
